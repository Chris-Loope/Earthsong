## Observable Cycles

Earthsong begins its practice of cycles with observation. Astronomy describes what the Sun, Earth, and Moon are doing. Reflection asks what a person might learn from paying attention. The second may be meaningful, but it does not alter the first.

No astronomical event dictates a decision, personality, mood, or future. A cycle can offer a reliable time to pause without becoming a force that controls human life.

### Why seasons turn

Earth's rotational axis is tilted about 23.4 degrees relative to its orbit around the Sun. As Earth travels around the Sun, each hemisphere alternately receives more direct light and longer days. The seasons are caused primarily by this tilt, not by Earth's changing distance from the Sun.

A **solstice** occurs when the Sun reaches its most northerly or southerly position in Earth's sky. The June solstice brings the greatest span of daylight to the Northern Hemisphere and the least to the Southern Hemisphere; the December solstice reverses those conditions. The event is one global instant with opposite seasonal meanings by hemisphere.

An **equinox** occurs when the centre of the Sun crosses Earth's equatorial plane. Day and night are nearly—but generally not exactly—equal. The Sun's visible disk and the bending of light through the atmosphere extend measured daylight, and local terrain changes what an observer sees.

**Earthsong reflection:** Solstices make limits visible; equinoxes make direction visible. Both can be used as checkpoints for what has changed, what conditions are present, and what one practical choice should carry into the coming season.

### Why the Moon changes shape

The Moon does not make its own visible light. Half is illuminated by the Sun, and the portion seen from Earth changes as the Moon orbits our planet. A complete sequence of phases takes about 29.5 days.

At **new moon**, the Moon is near the Sun's direction in the sky and its illuminated half faces mostly away from Earth. At **full moon**, Earth is between the directions of the Sun and Moon, so the near side appears fully illuminated. The waxing and waning phases are changing views of this sunlit half, not Earth's shadow passing over the Moon.

**Earthsong reflection:** A phase can be a prompt to notice change that is gradual, relational, and easy to overlook. Any personal meaning belongs to the observer; the Moon need not be assigned an intention.

### Why eclipses occur

The Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's path around the Sun, so the three bodies do not align at every new or full moon. A **solar eclipse** can occur at new moon when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun. A **lunar eclipse** can occur at full moon when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow.

Eclipses do not thin a veil or predict upheaval as matters of fact. They reveal orbital geometry through light and shadow. Eye safety is essential during a solar eclipse; outside the brief total phase of a total solar eclipse, direct viewing requires appropriate solar filters.

**Earthsong reflection:** Rarity can sharpen attention. The most durable response is not to invent a message, but to observe carefully, protect one another, and learn what the event makes visible.

### A practice of record

Choose one cycle and record the same observation repeatedly: sunrise position from one safe viewpoint, minutes of daylight, the Moon's shape and location, or a recurring event in one plant. Include the date, time, place, and conditions.

Write interpretation only after observation. Over time, the record becomes a conversation between the measured world and the life lived within it.

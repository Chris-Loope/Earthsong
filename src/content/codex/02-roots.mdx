## Living Lineages

No philosophy grows without influence. Earthsong has been shaped by ecology, astronomy, poetry, conservation, lived relationships with place, and conversations across many systems of thought. Acknowledging influence, however, is not permission to claim another community's identity, ceremony, symbol, or authority.

There is no single “Indigenous spirituality,” just as there is no single Native American, Aboriginal Australian, Māori, Andean, African, Asian, or European worldview. These names hold distinct peoples, histories, languages, lands, and living disagreements. Turning them into a list of universal nature teachings erases the very relationships Earthsong hopes to respect.

Earthsong therefore does not present other peoples' traditions as its ancient roots. It has no inherited right to combine them, conduct their ceremonies, rename their sacred places, or recruit their ancestors and spiritual beings into its own story.

### How Earthsong learns

Respectful learning is specific and accountable:

1. **Name the source.** Credit the nation, community, scholar, artist, or knowledge holder rather than attributing an idea to a continent or a broad identity.
2. **Speak in the present tense.** Peoples and traditions are living, changing, and self-defining—not remnants of an imagined past.
3. **Distinguish public from permitted.** Something visible online is not automatically ours to reproduce. Closed teachings, ceremonies, designs, stories, and places remain outside Earthsong unless their custodians clearly choose otherwise.
4. **Let people interpret themselves.** Use sources created or approved by the people being discussed. Do not make Earthsong the authority on someone else's way of life.
5. **Return value.** Learning should lead toward material respect: supporting community-led work, following access guidance, protecting habitat, paying for knowledge and art, and correcting errors.

Attribution is not a footnote added after borrowing. It changes what we choose to use in the first place.

### Begin where you stand

The most honest path into relationship is not to collect distant traditions. It is to learn the place that already supports your life.

Find the watershed beneath your address. Learn the names of several species that live nearby and what pressures they face. Learn which Indigenous peoples have longstanding and present relationships with the land, using sources those communities publish. Notice who currently cares for shared water, soil, trees, farms, parks, and streets. Follow local rules and community requests before deciding what respect should look like.

Knowledge does not create ownership. It creates obligations: to cite, to ask, to contribute, and sometimes to leave what is not ours alone.

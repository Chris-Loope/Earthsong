## The Practice of Place

Earthsong does not maintain a map of universal sacred sites. A place does not become available for someone else's spiritual use because it is beautiful, ancient, remote, or significant to the people who belong to it.

Some places are public. Some are private, fragile, dangerous, seasonally closed, ceremonial, or restricted by their custodians. Some can be visited without entering every story they hold. Respect begins by accepting that not every place is ours to enter, interpret, photograph, name, or share.

The alternative to collecting destinations is **place literacy**: sustained attention to the place where a person actually lives.

### Five ways to know a place

1. **Follow the water.** Learn the watershed, the source of tap water, the path of stormwater, and where wastewater goes.
2. **Read the living calendar.** Notice recurring leaf, flower, insect, bird, heat, frost, rain, and drought events. Record change rather than relying on a remembered “normal.”
3. **Learn human histories specifically.** Ask whose homelands these are, who was displaced, who remains, who arrived later, and who cares for the place now. Prefer sources communities create about themselves.
4. **Understand access.** Check ownership, public guidance, closures, permits, hazards, and requests from land managers and cultural custodians before visiting.
5. **Practise reciprocity.** Choose care the place actually needs: reduce runoff, remove litter where it is safe, support habitat, use less water, volunteer with accountable local work, or leave a sensitive area undisturbed.

### A visitor's ethic

When away from home, humility travels better than entitlement. Stay on permitted routes. Do not remove natural or cultural material. Do not copy a ceremony because it was witnessed. Ask before photographing people or sharing the location of vulnerable sites. Follow local direction even when its reason is not fully explained to you.

Leave behind the expectation that a place must provide revelation. Attention is not a transaction.

### A practice within reach

Choose a small place you can return to without trespass or harm: a street tree, drainage edge, garden, shoreline, park corner, or view from a window. Visit for twenty minutes. Record the date, weather, one living thing, one human-made feature, and one change since your last observation.

Then ask: **What would care for this place look like at my actual scale?**

The answer may be learning another name, changing one household habit, joining existing work, or doing nothing beyond allowing a living thing its space. Place becomes meaningful not when we declare it sacred, but when attention becomes an accountable relationship.
